# How strong is the claim, and what would refute it?

The argument for how strong the thesis claim is, positioned against the literature's weakest-link and minimum rules, and what would refute it. The claim and its scope boundary are `CLM-001` and `CLM-003`; the refusals are `CLM-002`.

- **Weaker than weakest-link:** the claim does not collapse the composed credibility to its worst input; errors at one stage can offset another (`LIT-008`).
  - The compensation is asserted with one example, not demonstrated; each paper establishes one clause and no other (`ARG-003`), so offset is a stated possibility, not a shown effect.
- **Not a minimum rule:** the literature aggregates credibility over process steps as a minimum (`LIT-008`); the claim instead locates loss or corruption at a named handover (`ARG-002`).
- **Construct:** the claim is the thesis's own, with no single cited source asserting it; the paper set supports it (`ARG-004`).
- **Refuted by detection:** a missing or corrupted clause that a later step detects by default, without being told to, refutes the claim.
  - The premise that loss is not detected downstream by default is an empirical claim about current tooling, and no check verifies it.
- **Refuted by redundancy:** a decision where the three clauses add nothing to the claim refutes it, scoped to the heterogeneous-CPS examined object (`ARG-006`).
  - Simple single-model decisions already satisfy the clauses, so the redundancy refutation holds only within the thesis's scope.

- The compensation argument and its asserted-not-demonstrated bound.
- The construct provenance.
- The two refutation conditions and their scopes.
